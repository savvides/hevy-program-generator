# Exercise evidence and programming policies

Reviewed 2026-11-02. The [November 2, 2026 Consensus review](research/2026-11-02-consensus-review.md)
is the primary research corpus for the topics it covers. Corrected identities and source roles live in the cache and
[`data/evidence_authority.json`](../data/evidence_authority.json).
The index distinguishes metadata checks, original abstracts and selected full-text
sections. This is a targeted evidence update, not a systematic appraisal of all
100 papers. Exported takeaways, citation counts and journal quartiles do not
establish a claim's validity.

Research findings inform decisions. Exact program settings remain coaching
choices unless a relevant source directly supports them. QA verifies structure,
citation membership and configured policies; it cannot establish optimal training,
individual recovery, study quality or clinical safety.

## Adult training findings

| Topic and source | Supported interpretation and population | Limits for programming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength, hypertrophy and function — [Currier et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897), row 1 | An ACSM overview of 137 reviews in healthy adults ≥18 supports progressive resistance training. Heavier loads favor strength; higher volume can favor hypertrophy. | Its broad guidance includes loads ≥80% 1RM and 2–3 sets for strength, and ≥10 weekly sets for hypertrophy. These are goal-specific findings, not universal pass/fail minimums. The journal full text was not independently retrieved; the original abstract and official ACSM summaries were checked. |
| Volume and frequency — [Pelland et al. 2026; online 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w), row 13 | Meta-regressions of 67 studies, 2,058 participants, mean age about 25, 79.1% male. More volume showed diminishing returns. Higher frequency favored strength; hypertrophy frequency effects were compatible with negligible differences. | Study-level associations do not identify an individual's optimum. Fractional indirect-set accounting had the strongest relative model fit; it does not validate every catalog weight. Predominantly young/male samples limit demographic extrapolation. |
| Proximity to failure — [Robinson et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2), row 15; [Grgic et al. 2022; online 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007), row 48 | Exploratory estimated-RIR models suggest hypertrophy benefits nearer failure, with less relationship to strength. The failure/non-failure synthesis does not establish failure as generally necessary. | RIR estimates, model uncertainty, volume control and predominantly young-adult failure studies matter. Neither source establishes exact RPE 8 or mandatory accessory failure. |
| Higher-volume comparisons — [Baz-Valle et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2022-0017), row 62 | Seven studies in trained young men aged 18–35; moderate versus high volume effects differed by muscle. | The proposed 12–20 sets is population-limited. It is not an 18-set age ceiling, individual MRV, or mandatory minimum for an aerobic-primary plan. |
| Concurrent training — [Huiberts et al. 2024; online 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9), row 67 | Across 59 studies in healthy adults 18–50, lower-body strength interference appeared in male but not female subgroup estimates. | Female hypertrophy data and highly strength-trained samples were absent. Subgroup results do not show women are interference-free or justify sex-specific recovery hours. The broader Schumann supplement below also informs this topic. |
| Cardiovascular health — [Paluch et al. 2024, “2023 Update”](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001189), row 37 | The AHA statement describes cardiovascular and risk-factor benefits in adults with and without cardiovascular disease. | Many trials were short. A statement covering clinical populations does not clear a particular routine for an individual. |

The corpus also contains load, frequency, exercise-system, sarcopenia and health
papers indexed without claim-level appraisal. Their inclusion does not make every
exported conclusion a program rule. For example, [Zhang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2025-110503)
(row 38) is a prospective-cohort analysis whose identity was verified but original
content remained inaccessible. Its exported mortality associations cannot establish
a causal weekly training-time cap. The selected ACSM 2009 stand (row 52; export
incorrectly says 2011) is historical context; the 2026 update leads current adult guidance.

## Age, reported sex and life-stage applicability

These are the studies' age definitions, not interchangeable biological thresholds.
Age 40, 60 or 65 does not trigger a universal change in training dose. “Male,”
“female,” “men” and “women” below describe the papers' reported participant groups;
those labels do not establish participants' gender identity or hormone exposure.

| Context and actual study scope | Finding and limit |
| --- | --- |
| Youth: [Komm et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02499-0), row 19, participants <18; [Stricker et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2020-1011), row 79, children/adolescents | The youth synthesis reports hypertrophy, with comparator-dependent maturity results and sparse female active-control evidence. The AAP report emphasizes qualified supervision, technique, competency and progressive loading. No adult-bodybuilding dose or sex/maturity multiplier follows. The repository's adult generator stops for an explicitly supplied age <18; this is a software scope boundary, not a prohibition on supervised youth training. |
| Adults 18–45: [Refalo et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19042), row 5 | In 29 matched-program studies, absolute hypertrophy slightly favored males while relative changes were similar. This supports comparable relative potential, not proof of identical optimal doses. Highly trained, pregnant, frail and hormone-therapy populations cannot be inferred from this sample. |
| Adults >50: [Jones et al. 2021; online 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01388-4), row 6 | Across 30 studies, women gained more relative lower-body strength; relative upper-body strength and muscle-size changes did not differ significantly. Men gained more absolute strength/size. These outcomes do not supply numeric sex-dependent prescriptions. |
| Older-adult eligibility ≥55; sample-age summaries 59.0–88.5 (means when available): [Straight et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00170.2020), row 7 | Resistance training increased type I/II fiber size, with similar male/female responses. Study-level age/intensity associations cannot establish individual load reductions, and cellular outcomes are not all whole-muscle or functional outcomes. |
| Adults >60: [Kneffel et al. 2021; online 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2020.1822595), row 65 | Higher frequency had a small positive strength association; hypertrophy frequency effects were nonsignificant. The authors' cautious discussion does not establish a twice-weekly maximum. |
| Very old adults ≥75, with ≥80 subgroup: [Grgic et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01331-7), row 20 | Strength and whole-muscle size improved. Handgrip and fiber-size comparisons were nonsignificant. Limited adverse-event reporting does not provide universal safety assurance or an exact dose. |
| Frailty/sarcopenia contexts: [Tan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s11556-025-00399-2), row 89, older adults with sarcopenia; [Jacob et al. 2026; online 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-025-01877-2), row 29, 19 pre/frail women, mean age 77.5 | Tan reports strength/function improvements, but muscle-quality evidence involved only two studies. Its cumulative-duration regressions are exploratory and outcome-specific; 2,716 minutes is not a required dose. Jacob randomized leucine versus alanine during training/diet support, not training versus no training. Frailty is individual clinical context, not a synonym for age or an automatic dose formula. |

### Menstrual cycles and menopause

The cycle papers disagree. [Colenso-Semple et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542)
(row 23) finds inconsistent results and poor cycle verification, making strong
hormone-based training conclusions premature. [D'Souza et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000004031)
(row 22) found no significant scheduling advantage across three cycles in 24
healthy eumenorrheic women using a unilateral quadriceps model. Short duration,
broad phase categories and incomplete biopsy outcomes limit generalization.

[Da Silva Faria et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.47197/retos.v83.119754)
(row 100) favors phase-based periodization while acknowledging conflicting results,
small samples and heterogeneous protocols. Its publisher abstract and references
were checked; its full PDF was inaccessible. It follows PRISMA-ScR and does not
list row 22 among its displayed references. Retain this disagreement. Continuous
programming with individual symptom/preference adjustments remains a coaching
starting point; no automatic menstrual-phase dose rule follows. These findings do
not settle oral-contraceptive, amenorrhea or menopause questions.

[Isenmann et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-023-02671-y)
(row 25) studied healthy women 40–60: 10 control weeks followed by 10 training
weeks, with 31 completers. Only postmenopausal participants were randomized between
intensities. Strength improved in all groups; the proposed need for more than 6–8
sets after menopause was a hypothesis, not a tested higher-volume intervention.
[González-Gálvez et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2024.2310521)
(row 96) supports strength/fitness benefits across 12 trials, with more uncertainty
for bone and body-composition outcomes. Neither establishes a menopause-specific minimum.

The [LIFTMOR trial, Watson et al. 2018; online 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.3284)
(row 83) improved bone/functional outcomes in screened postmenopausal women with
low bone mass, mean age about 65, under close supervision. One minor lower-back
spasm occurred; the export's “without adverse events” is inaccurate. The trial
cannot provide unsupervised osteoporosis-program clearance.

### Pregnancy, postpartum and gender-diverse participants

[Prevett et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-109123)
(row 78) supports benefits of studied prenatal interventions, but 90% of programs
combined modalities and resistance doses were generally low/moderate with limited
reporting. It does not establish isolated resistance-training causation, a universal
RPE, or clearance for every heavy routine. [Beamish et al. 2025; online 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108619)
(row 91) concerns the first postpartum year: pelvic-floor training reduced some
pelvic-floor outcomes; abdominal-training evidence for inter-rectus distance had
lower certainty. These interventions do not establish a heavy-lifting return timetable.

[Cheung et al. 2024; online 2023](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgad414)
(row 44) reviews mainly nonathletic transgender participants from small uncontrolled
or cross-sectional studies. Therapy and measured endpoint matter; feminizing and
masculinizing hormone therapy cannot be summarized as one direction of performance
change. Gender identity alone does not establish physiology or a training multiplier.
[Linsenmeyer et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cdnut.2024.103706)
(row 45) has a blank exported abstract and inaccessible original content. It remains
unverified for substantive claims. A related case series with a different DOI is
not a substitute corpus record.

## Retained coaching policies

| Setting | Purpose and limits |
| --- | --- |
| 3–18 weighted weekly sets; at least one weekly exposure | Screening bounds for this repository, not individual MEV/MRV, a hypertrophy optimum or an age ceiling. Review goals, direct sets, overlap, tolerance and progression together. The active plan has an aerobic primary goal. |
| Advisory push/pull and knee/hip ratios 0.8–1.2 | Descriptive balance targets, not pass/fail gates or proven injury-prevention bounds. |
| Indirect-set weight 0.5 | Bookkeeping heuristic. Pelland supports considering fractional sets, but does not validate each movement-registry assignment. |
| Heavy-slot RPE ≤8, solo sets, `[5, 5, 3]` warmups | Exact coaching choices. The legacy `compound` flag selects this policy, not anatomical classification. RPE 8 approximates two repetitions in reserve; estimates vary. Warmup weights are not calculated by the export. |
| Existing rest targets | Starting targets; extend them to preserve performance. Review volume or time budgets if longer rests are needed. Never shorten rest or omit warmups to make the timing model pass. |
| 48-hour lower-body/interval buffer | Configured start-to-start scheduling choice, including weekly wraparound. Missing local `start_time` makes hourly spacing provisional. It is not a universal recovery guarantee. |
| 25% interval-work review ceiling | A screening choice: interval-work minutes divided by total scheduled cardio minutes. Keep explicit easy time, interval work and unclassified warmup/recovery separate. No universal 80/20-minute split or 20-minute interval floor follows. |

Accessory effort targets do not require failure; stop at the intended technique
limit. Exercise choice follows elicited equipment and joint constraints. Registry
joint tags are selection labels, not injury guarantees. Prior work does not
necessarily warm every accessory. Exact exercise doses, deload timing and this
athlete's cycling choice remain coaching decisions.

Optional explicit `age_years`, `sex` and `primary_goal` metadata provide context;
omitted values remain unknown. Do not infer them from a title or focus string, or
alter sets, loads, rest, calories or RPE from demographic labels. Life-stage and
clinical needs require individual assessment; source membership does not resolve them.

## Explicit supplemental exercise sources

The manifest retains these eight sources for program citations because the selected
corpus does not replace their specific questions. They remain supplements, with
claim-level limits; ordinary cache hits or document mentions do not grant this role.

| Source | Retained scope |
| --- | --- |
| [Andersen et al. 2017; print 2018](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001826) | Acute deadlift/hex-bar/hip-thrust EMG comparison in 13 trained men; not long-term hypertrophy or injury prevention. |
| [Collings et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003091) | Modeled gluteal forces in eight specific exercises in 14 female footballers aged 18–32; the study did not test machine hip abduction or lateral lunges; not clinical safety or individual injury-prevention proof. |
| [Maeo et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002523) | Seated versus prone leg-curl hypertrophy; apply only within the exercise/population comparison. |
| [McGill 2010](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0b013e3181df4521) | General trunk training and tolerance; not validation of an exact carry/Pallof/twist dose or spinal protection. |
| [Milanović et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0365-0) | Average VO2max benefit of interval and continuous training; no exact weekly floor, ceiling or validation of this athlete's interval dose. |
| [Buchheit & Laursen 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0029-x) | Interactions among interval intensity, duration, recovery and frequency. Prescribed interval minutes are not measured time at VO2max. |
| [Seiler 2010](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276) | Low-intensity emphasis in endurance athletes; session-based observations are not universal minute percentages for recreational concurrent training. |
| [Schumann et al. 2022; online 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7) | Concurrent training generally preserved maximal strength/hypertrophy; explosive strength was more affected, especially in same-session training. No universal 48-hour rule or modality ban. |

## Practical review and document precedence

Record comparable sessions' warmups, working load/reps/RPE, both unilateral sides,
rest extensions, elapsed time and symptoms. Review repeatable performance trends
before adding work or changing recovery. A short session alone does not show an
insufficient stimulus. On the bike, record completed intervals, pace/power, heart
rate and session RPE. This is a coaching review process, not a validated outcome test.

The [nutrition document](nutrition_evidence_base.md) covers fueling and energy
limits. The [review index](research/2026-11-02-consensus-review.md) replaces the
old sprawling reference list as this review's corpus register. Earlier bibliographies,
specifications and audits remain in Git history; they do not override this dated
guidance. Generate the current readable plan from Python with `scripts/render_program.py`.
