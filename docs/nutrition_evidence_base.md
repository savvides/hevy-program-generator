# Nutrition evidence and individual planning

Reviewed 2026-11-02. The [November 2, 2026 Consensus corpus and review index](research/2026-11-02-consensus-review.md)
lead the topics they cover. The index records all primary papers and separates
bibliographic checks from original abstracts and selected full-text sections.
Exported takeaways are discovery summaries, not verified instructions. This update
does not claim a systematic appraisal of every paper or prescribe a new diet.

Daily targets remain individual planning choices. The research does not establish
an age-40 switch, automatic sex-specific macros or a hormone-based dose formula.
Research participant sex labels do not establish gender identity or hormone exposure.
The [exercise document](evidence_base.md) covers lifespan and life-stage training limits.

## Protein: useful adult targets, uncertain individual optima

[Morton et al. 2018; online 2017](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)
(row 4) pooled 49 trials in 1,863 healthy adults, with resistance training lasting
at least six weeks. Energy-restricted participants were excluded. Additional
protein modestly improved adaptations; the estimated intake breakpoint was
1.62 g/kg/day, with a wide 95% confidence interval of 1.03–2.20. It is a group-level
estimate, not a binary anabolic switch, intake ceiling or medical minimum.
About 1.6 g/kg/day of total body mass can guide adult resistance-training planning;
1.6–2.2 g/kg/day is a coaching option that accommodates uncertainty. This analysis
does not establish deficit-specific targets or a separate requirement after age 40.

[Nunes et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcsm.12922) (row 26) included
healthy, non-obese adults and excluded weight-loss diets and disease. Its age
subgroups were <65 and ≥65. Additional protein produced small benefits, mainly
with resistance exercise. The older subgroup receiving 1.2–1.59 g/kg/day became
nonsignificant after one trial was excluded; the dataset contained no older-adult
resistance-exercise trials at ≥1.6 g/kg/day. This gap cannot establish a lower
optimum for older adults or justify reducing protein automatically after 65.

| Specific population/source | Interpretation and limit |
| --- | --- |
| [Kirwan et al. 2022; online 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqab355), row 27; study mean ages ≥50, including sarcopenic/frail participants | Protein with resistance exercise improved appendicular lean mass and handgrip in pooled subsets. Lean-mass effects appeared only in sarcopenic/frail subgroups; heterogeneity and risk of bias matter. This is not a dose rule for all adults over 40 or 50. |
| [Jacob et al. 2026; online 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-025-01877-2), row 29; 19 pre/frail women, mean age 77.5 | During 12 weeks of training and an approximately 1.2 g/kg/day diet, leucine did not add benefit versus alanine. Both groups improved, but there was no randomized no-training comparator. Neither an optimal daily target nor compulsory meal-level leucine follows. |
| [Holwerda et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxy169), row 30; 41 active older men, mean age about 70 | Protein after exercise and before sleep did not add mass/strength gains over 12 weeks. This counters compulsory supplement timing; it does not show timing can never matter. |

Whole foods can support dietary variety; powders can help meet a target conveniently.
No reviewed finding requires an 80–90% whole-food quota. Acute protein-synthesis
responses do not automatically establish long-term hypertrophy equivalence or a
universal meal/leucine requirement. Retained meal-distribution context appears below.

## Creatine: optional adult support with population limits

[Kreider et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z)
(row 98) describes established creatine-monohydrate protocols. A practical optional
adult maintenance choice is 3–5 g/day. Loading is optional; starting with daily
maintenance dosing increases stores more slowly. Supplement use is not a program gate.

[Wang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu16213665) (row 31) pooled
23 studies in healthy adults **<50**. The sample contained substantially more men
than women. The male subgroup's significant strength effect and the small female
subgroup's nonsignificant effect do not establish a significant sex difference;
the upper-body subgroup contrast was only a trend. Do not label creatine ineffective
for women or prescribe different doses by sex from this analysis.

[Yao et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1919884) (row 32)
included 11 trials in adults ≥60, primarily generally healthy participants with
some vulnerable postmenopausal groups. The additional strength effect was small
and of moderate certainty; mass and physical-function benefits were uncertain.
Sex, loading, dose and duration were not stable moderators. These results do not
promise sarcopenia reversal or establish a separate older-adult dose formula.

[Longobardi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1682746)
(row 49) reports occasional gastrointestinal distress, particularly at higher doses.
The export's stronger “no GI effects” interpretation is inaccurate. Controlled
studies generally do not show impaired kidney function in healthy participants,
but pregnancy and pre-existing kidney conditions lack adequate evidence. Healthy-adult
evidence is not individual clinical clearance or proof of no adverse effects.

The large [Kreider et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2488937)
and [Gonzalez et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports14040137)
safety analyses (rows 53 and 60; 685 and 684 trials) substantially reuse the same
database. They are not independent replications. Overall participant adverse-event
frequency was not significantly higher in the earlier analysis; incomplete event,
dose and demographic reporting limits interpretation and cannot exclude rare harms.

[Jagim & Kerksick 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13020664)
(row 64) describes a narrower youth evidence base. At the review's date, dedicated
safety-primary studies in healthy adolescent athletes were absent. That is a
time-bounded statement, not a fresh claim that no studies exist in 2026. Lack of
self-reported adverse events does not confirm safety; adult supplementation defaults
do not automatically apply to minors.

## Energy deficit, energy availability and dietary patterns

[Murphy & Koehler 2022; online 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14075)
(row 35) found that energy restriction impaired lean-mass gains during resistance
training, while pooled strength gains were not significantly different. Its roughly
500 kcal/day meta-regression estimate concerns loss of lean-mass gains, not a
clinical safety threshold. Experienced lifters were poorly represented, some
comparisons matched separate studies, protein intake could not be modeled, and
energy availability could not be calculated. A smaller deficit is not a guarantee
of lean-mass preservation; a larger deficit is not automatically unsafe by this
study's definition. No automatic calorie change follows.

[Gallant et al. 2025; online 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02130-0)
(row 36) reviews athlete low energy availability (LEA) and relative energy deficiency
in sport. Associations with performance and bone health warrant attention, but
heterogeneous definitions and samples limit individual prediction. Reported sex-group
prevalence is not a universal risk multiplier. Energy deficit and LEA are related
but different concepts: the tracker estimates energy balance, not energy remaining
after exercise relative to fat-free mass, and does not diagnose LEA or REDs.

[Granic et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11040745) (row 84)
is an overview of earlier reviews plus an observational update, mainly in older
adults; it is not a dietary-treatment trial. [Papadopoulou et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15051104)
(row 95) covers apparently healthy older adults, described as >65 in its title:
10 observational studies and no clinical trials. Healthier/Mediterranean dietary
patterns were associated with some mass, function or mobility outcomes; strength
results were mixed and evidence did not establish sarcopenia prevention or treatment.
Confounding, dietary scoring and differing outcome definitions limit causal claims.
These papers do not justify a mandatory food quota.

[Everett 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17172792) (row 92) addresses
adolescent athletes' growth, development and training energy needs. It is a narrative
review, not validation of adult deficit calculations in youth. Avoid automatic
restriction or age/sex macro formulas. The gender-diverse athlete record
[Linsenmeyer et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cdnut.2024.103706)
(row 45) remains unavailable for substantive review; it cannot support dosing
formulas and must not be replaced with a related publication's different DOI.

## Retained supplemental context outside the selected corpus

The following guidance was already qualified in the September 20 review and remains
context for topics not adequately covered by the reviewed corpus findings. It has
not received a fresh comprehensive appraisal in this update. These specific papers
are **not** designated-corpus records, even when an author also appears in the
corpus. Mention here does not add them to the manifest's eight approved program-citation
supplements or authorize other background cache records.

- Meal distribution: [Moore et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/glu103)
  and [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1)
  discuss per-meal dose and distribution. About 0.4 g/kg at a meal is a planning
  option, not a binary anabolic switch or a universal 35–45 g/3 g leucine rule
  after age 40. The directly reviewed Holwerda study above qualifies compulsory
  post-exercise or pre-sleep supplementation; a fixed bedtime bolus is not required.
- Protein source: [McKendry et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajcnut.2024.05.009)
  concerns short-term integrated synthesis in older men. It cannot establish
  equal long-term hypertrophy across every food matrix or a whole-food quota.
- Carbohydrate: [Henselmans et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856)
  found little clear extra benefit in fed resistance sessions up to about 10
  sets per muscle. [King et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35809162/)
  found benefit more likely with longer sessions or longer fasting. Match intake
  to workload, energy, tolerance and performance; no universal lifting duration
  threshold, 30–60 g/hour mandate or 5–8 g/kg/day requirement follows.
- Short recovery windows: Alghannam et al. 2018 and van Loon et al. 2000 address
  rapid glycogen repletion after demanding exercise. Their protocols are not a
  default hourly-feeding requirement for separated weekly sessions.
- Fed/fasted exercise: [Aird et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13054)
  found pre-exercise feeding benefited prolonged aerobic performance, with no
  clear shorter-effort benefit in that analysis. Fueling key sessions is a
  practical option; this does not require every heavy/interval session to be fed.
  [Schoenfeld et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-014-0054-7)
  does not support fasting as necessary for fat loss.
- Antioxidants: [Wyckelsma et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP288638)
  is a three-week sprint-interval trial in young men. Vitamin C/E altered muscle
  proteome responses; this is not proof of universal strength, hypertrophy or
  VO2max loss, an exact harmful threshold, or a safe timing window.
  [Peternelj & Coombes 2011](https://doi.org/10.2165/11594400-000000000-00000)
  reviews mixed findings. Routine high-dose supplements are not an established
  performance aid; clinical indications need individual advice. Whole foods and
  concentrated supplements are different exposures.

The former 99-paper nutrition bibliography remains in Git history as background.
Its entries do not silently become current policy evidence. A future authority
update may deliberately incorporate additional sources after claim-level review.

## This repository's tracker

`scripts/track_deficit.py` is an arithmetic planning aid. Wearable energy estimates,
consumer body-composition measurements, the 3,500 kcal/lb conversion and a
constant-lean-mass projection are uncertain approximations. Its protein multiplier
uses estimated lean mass, whereas the adult protein findings above use total body
mass. The multiplier of 1.18 g/lb estimated lean mass (about 2.6 g/kg estimated lean
mass), fat at 70 g/day and the selected 0.55 lb/week loss rate remain personal
choices. This review does not change them.

Deadline arithmetic remains separate from the chosen rate. The tracker does not change the chosen deficit automatically as a date approaches
and does not prescribe calorie changes from short-term scale movement. Finite-input, body-fat-fraction
and macro-feasibility checks verify arithmetic, not nutritional adequacy, suitable
energy availability or medical safety.

Trend calculations require at least three distinct dates across 14 days, average
same-day readings and use the median of pairwise slopes. This is a modest data
quality rule, not proof of tissue change. Review several weeks of consistent
measurements, intake, hunger, recovery and training performance before an individual
planning adjustment. No age, sex or deadline label establishes a suitable deficit.
